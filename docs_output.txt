[42] Automated monitoring systems significantly improve the enforcement of traffic regulations by providing accurate and real-time detection. One of the most critical benefits is the reduction of human intervention, which minimizes errors in ticket generation and ensures a more transparent enforcement process (Akshit et al., 2025). High accuracy rates—exceeding 98% in some deep learning models—demonstrate the potential for these systems to outperform human observers (Abd Allah, 2023). Furthermore, the ability to generate "e-challans" delivered via SMS or email promotes immediate accountability and encourages more disciplined driving behavior (Singh, 2024).
[44] Modern traffic violation detection systems are defined by their use of image processing software routines to identify vehicles and assess compliance with specific road rules (Tan, 2013). Their purpose is to enhance road safety through the use of surveillance video feeds, particularly at intersections where signal violations are common (Gehani et al., 2024). These systems aim to create an intelligent traffic management environment that integrates violation detection, license plate localization, and character recognition into a single automated workflow (Uy et al., 2016). OpenCV (Open Source Computer Vision) is a famous open-source computer vision and machine learning library widely used in various fields, including robotics. One of the typical applications of OpenCV in autonomous robotics is lane detection, where the robot uses a camera to navigate along a lane. 
[45] To detect black lanes in autonomous robots, OpenCV provides several functions and algorithms that can be used. The following is a brief overview of the steps involved in using OpenCV for lane detection in autonomous robots. The first step is to capture the image of the lane using a camera mounted on the robot. This image is then used as input for the lane detection algorithm. The image must be preprocessed to enhance its quality before detecting the lanes. This can include resizing the image, adjusting the brightness and contrast, and applying filters such as Gaussian blur to remove noise. Next, the Canny edge detection algorithm is applied to the preprocessed image to extract the edges in the picture. This helps to identify the boundaries of the lanes. Since the robot only needs to navigate along the lane, a Region of Interest (ROI) is selected to focus on the area where the roads are present. This helps reduce the processing time and improve the detection accuracy. The Hough transform algorithm is then applied to the edge-detected image within the ROI to detect lines. This helps to identify the straight lines that represent the lanes.

[60] Challenges in Traffic Violation Monitoring
[61] A significant research gap remains regarding the performance of AI models under extreme weather or varying lighting conditions (Arun, 2025; Rose et al., 2025). Systems may struggle with false positives in violation detection, which can lead to unjust penalties and a loss of public trust (Malik et al., 2023). Furthermore, the scalability of these models in areas with extremely high traffic density or diverse urban settings is often unexplored in current studies (Akshit et al., 2025; Kaur et al., 2025).The integration of automated systems with existing legal frameworks remains a major hurdle, particularly concerning the privacy of personal data captured in public spaces (Rose et al., 2025). Ethical concerns regarding automated surveillance and the potential for "digital authoritarianism" are frequently cited as barriers to public acceptance (Arun, 2025). To combat this, some systems have introduced face-blurring modules to preserve individual privacy while still identifying the vehicle involved in the infraction (Rose et al., 2025).
[63] The crucial problem of road safety in India has prompted the creation of integrated systems that incorporate "penalty points" to promote compliance and automate the issuance of penalties (Tulasidasu et al., 2025). Researchers are employing YOLO-based deep learning to address the unique issues of controlling high-density, chaotic traffic in cities like Dhaka (Kusumaningtyas et al., 2025). These case studies show that the fundamental requirement for automated surveillance and transparent fine issuing is ubiquitous across emerging urban contexts, even though the specific infractions may differ. The significance of a scalable and modular architecture that makes it simple to incorporate additional detection modules when traffic patterns change is a key takeaway (Rose et al., 2025; Deshpande et al., 2026). According to studies, detection accuracy is greatly increased when extensive datasets, such the COCO dataset, are used for initial model training (Gehani et al., 2024). Furthermore, modern neural network techniques may achieve over 90% accuracy in both detection and recognition, according to the shift to deep learning (Uy et al., 2016; Abd Allah, 2023).
[64] Future Directions for Traffic Violations Monitoring in Zimbabwe
[65] By combining automated ticketing systems with current law enforcement infrastructure, future systems should concentrate on closing the gap between detection and real-time notification (Thamilarasi et al., 2025). The sustainability and long-term upkeep of these technologies in regional settings must also be studied (Abd Allah, 2023). More long-term research is clearly needed to determine how well these systems actually reduce infractions over a number of years (Thamilarasi et al., 2025). The use of Large Language Models (LLMs) to produce structured, legally enforceable reports based on visual evidence is one potential breakthrough that could guarantee that infractions are recorded in a fashion that is admissible in court (Kaur et al., 2025). For 24-hour urban monitoring, the use of various sensors—such as RGB and thermal cameras—will probably become the norm (Gambhire et al., 2025). Additionally, the next stage in automated enforcement is represented by the introduction of "smart" surveillance components that may adjust based on changing traffic patterns and real-world usage (Deshpande et al., 2026).
